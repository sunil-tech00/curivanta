Is My Solar Quote a Fair Price? How to Check the Price per Watt

Solar quotes come in every size and shape, which makes them hard to compare. Price per watt cuts through that: one number that tells you whether you're paying a fair price for the system itself.

Updated October 2026 · 6 min read · By Your Solar Advisor

Short answer

Divide the cash price by the system size in watts. Most US home solar quotes land around $2.50–$3.50 per watt. Above $3.50, ask why, and get a second quote before you sign.

How to calculate it

You need two numbers from the quote: the system size (in kW) and the cash price.

Price per watt = cash price ÷ (system size in kW × 1,000)

Example

An 8 kW system quoted at $24,000: $24,000 ÷ 8,000 W = $3.00 per watt. That's within the typical range.

The same system quoted at $33,600 works out to $4.20 per watt: $9,600 more for identical capacity.

Two rules make the number meaningful:

  • Use the cash price, not the loan amount. Loan amounts often include a dealer fee of 10–30%, which makes a fair system look expensive (or hides an expensive one). See our guide to solar dealer fees.
  • Take the battery out first. A battery adds cost but no solar capacity. A $40,000 quote that includes a $15,000 battery is really $25,000 for 8 kW of solar: $3.13 per watt, not the alarming $5.00 you'd get by dividing the whole price.

What's a fair price per watt?

Prices vary by state, installer and equipment, but most US residential quotes fall in a fairly narrow band.

Price per wattWhat it usually means
Under $2.50Low. A good deal if the equipment, installer and warranties are solid. Check what's left out (permits, electrical upgrades, roof work).
$2.50 – $3.50The typical range for most US home systems.
$3.50 – $4.00High. Ask for an itemized price and get at least one competing quote.
Over $4.00Very high, unless there's genuine extra work such as a main panel upgrade, roof repairs or a complex install.

What legitimately moves the price

  • System size. Permits, design and setup cost about the same for any system, so small systems cost more per watt.
  • Equipment. Premium panels and microinverters or optimizers cost more than basic string inverters, sometimes worth it on shaded or complex roofs.
  • The roof and the site. Tile or metal roofs, several roof faces, steep pitches and ground mounts all add labor.
  • Electrical work. An older main panel may need an upgrade, which can add a few thousand dollars. It should be its own line item.
  • Local costs. Labor, permitting and inspection costs differ a lot from one area to the next.

What doesn't justify a higher price

  • Tax credit math. The 30% federal credit for homeowner-owned systems (Section 25D) ended for systems installed after December 31, 2025. A quote that shows a “net cost after the 30% credit” on a system you'd own is counting money you can't claim.
  • “Today only” pricing. A legitimate price is still there next week. Pressure to sign on the spot is a sales tactic, not a discount.
  • Financing baked in. If the “cash price” is the same as the loan amount, ask whether a dealer fee is included in both.

Price per watt is step one, not the whole story

Price per watt tells you what you're paying for capacity. What you really care about is what you pay for the electricity the system produces over its life. Two 8 kW systems at the same price can produce different amounts depending on roof direction, shade and equipment, and a quote can overstate production to make the savings look better.

So once the price per watt checks out, compare each quote's expected annual production (kWh) with an independent estimate, and work out the cost per kWh over 25 years. Red flags in a quote

  • No cash price on the quote, only a monthly payment.
  • Price per watt above $3.50 with no itemized explanation.
  • Savings that subtract a 30% federal tax credit for a system you'd own.
  • A system much larger than your usage (more than about 120% of what you use in a year).
  • Pressure to sign the same day to “lock in” a price.
