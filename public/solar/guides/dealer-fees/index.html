Solar Dealer Fees: The Hidden Cost Inside “Low-Rate” Solar Loans

A 2.99% solar loan sounds like a great deal when mortgage rates are much higher. The catch is usually a dealer fee: a charge you never see as a line item, but pay interest on for years.

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read · By Your Solar Advisor

Short answer

A dealer fee is what the lender charges the installer to offer a low interest rate, and the installer adds it to your loan amount. It's commonly 10–30%. Find it by comparing the loan amount with the cash price, then work out the true interest rate on what you actually received.

How dealer fees work

When market rates are 7% or more, a lender can't offer 2.99% for free. Instead, it charges the installer an upfront fee (often 10–30% of the loan) to “buy down” the rate. The installer covers that fee by raising the price of the system when you finance it.

The result: the loan has a low rate, but on a bigger balance. The fee rarely appears as a line item. It shows up as a gap between the cash price and the loan amount.

How to find it in your quote

  1. Ask for the cash price and the loan amount in writing. If the quote only shows a monthly payment, ask for both.
  2. Dealer fee = loan amount − cash price.
  3. Check the cash price itself against other installers using price per watt. Some installers build the fee into every price, so the cash price looks inflated too.
Example

Cash price: $24,000. Loan: $30,000 at 2.99% for 25 years.

  • Dealer fee: $6,000, or 25% on top of the cash price.
  • Payment: $142 a month; $42,632 over 25 years.
  • True interest rate on the $24,000 you actually received: 5.13%, not 2.99%.

Is a dealer fee always a bad deal?

Not always, and anyone who says otherwise hasn't run the numbers. In the example, the true rate is 5.13%. If the best alternative is a 6.99% loan on the $24,000 cash price ($169 a month, $50,842 over 25 years), the dealer-fee loan is actually cheaper if you keep it for the full 25 years.

The true interest rate is the number to compare. Put it next to what a credit union, home equity loan or HELOC would charge you on the cash price.

When a dealer fee really hurts: paying off early

The fee is charged on day one, so you pay all of it even if you keep the loan for only a few years. And many people do: they sell the house, refinance, or pay the loan down.

Notice the balance, too: after three years you'd still owe $27,466 on a system whose cash price was $24,000. Solar loans are usually secured with a filing on the equipment (a UCC-1 fixture filing), which generally has to be paid off or transferred when you sell, so a high balance can complicate a home sale.

Watch for loans that assume a lump-sum payment

Many solar loans were designed around the 30% federal tax credit: the low payment you were shown assumed you'd pay the credit toward the loan within the first 12–18 months. If you didn't, the payment rose.

The homeowner credit ended for systems installed after December 31, 2025. If a loan you're offered still assumes a lump-sum prepayment, the payment on the quote isn't the payment you'll actually have. In the example above, a $30,000 loan shown at $99 a month “with prepayment” becomes $142 a month without it.

Ask directly: “Does this payment assume a prepayment? What is the payment if I never make one?”

How to avoid overpaying

  • Always get the cash price, even if you plan to finance.
  • Get a quote from your bank or credit union on the cash price and compare its rate with the dealer-fee loan's true rate.
  • Ask about other loan options. Lenders usually offer several rate-and-fee combinations; a slightly higher rate often comes with a much smaller fee.
  • Think about how long you'll keep it. If you might sell or pay off within 5–10 years, a lower fee usually beats a lower rate.

Red flags

  • The quote shows a monthly payment but no cash price.
  • The loan amount is more than 15% above the cash price.
  • A payment that assumes you'll make a lump-sum prepayment.
  • “There's no fee,” while the loan amount is higher than the cash price.
