It is Saturday, 10 AM. Your colorist's client just cancelled, and the chair sits empty until noon. The stylist is ready, the color is mixed, the lights are on, and nobody is paying for any of it. By the time you find someone to take the slot, the morning is gone.

That is the cost of a last-minute cancellation. Not the inconvenience. The chair. An empty chair is money that never comes back, because you cannot sell Saturday at 10 AM on Monday.

## The cost of one empty chair a day

Do the math with your own numbers. This is an illustration, so swap in yours: one empty hour a day, at a $65 ticket, five days a week, is $325 a week. That is about $1,300 a month, roughly a week of a stylist's pay, gone to gaps in the book.

It does not take a bad week to get there. One cancellation a day is a normal week in most salons: a sick day, a work meeting that moved, a client who forgot and only remembered when the reminder landed. The chair does not care why it is empty. It costs you the same either way.

## Why a stricter policy does not fix it

The usual answer is a stricter cancellation policy: 48 hours' notice, a fee for late cancellations, a card on file. It sounds like it should work. In practice it does two things, and neither fills the chair.

First, it scares off good clients. The regular who has never missed an appointment reads a cancellation fee as a salon that does not trust them, and starts looking at the salon down the street that does not make them sign a policy before they can book a trim.

Second, it does not create a replacement booking. A fee might recover some of the lost ticket, if you can collect it and the client does not dispute it. But the chair is still empty, the stylist is still waiting, and the next client is still not in the book.

A policy protects you after the fact. It never fills the gap.

## The waitlist that actually works

The fix is not a policy. It is a list of people who already wanted that slot, and a way to reach them before it goes cold.

Most salons have a waitlist in name only: a notebook at the front desk, or a mental note that "a few people wanted Saturday." That is not a waitlist. A waitlist that works is specific: who asked, for which stylist, which service, and which day or time.

Build it at the moment of booking. When a client asks for a Saturday morning with Maya and the book is full, that is the moment to ask: "Want me to text you if Saturday morning opens up?" Write down the answer with the details (Maya, color, Saturday morning), not just the name. A list of names with no context is a list you will never use.

Keep it short and fresh. Ten people who asked for this week beat a hundred names from three months ago. When a slot is filled or the week passes, clear it out. A stale waitlist is worse than no waitlist, because it makes you feel covered when you are not.

## Speed matters more than anything

When a cancellation lands, the slot has a shelf life measured in minutes. The client who takes it is the one who hears about it first and can say yes in one reply.

That is why texting beats calling down a list. A phone call is a conversation, and by the time you have had three of them the slot is gone. A text says the one thing that matters, "Maya has Saturday at 10 AM open, want it?", and the client answers yes or no in seconds.

Text the most likely person first, not everyone at once. The client who asked for that stylist and that day is the one who will say yes. If they pass, move to the next name. One text at a time, in order, beats a group message that leaves two clients arguing over the same slot.

And start right away. A cancellation at 9:40 AM for a 10 AM slot is already a lost cause if you wait until the afternoon to start. The gap is filled in the first ten minutes, or it is not filled at all.

## Prevention: let clients move instead of ghosting

Filling gaps is half the job. The other half is getting fewer of them.

The clients who no-show are often not trying to waste your time. They forgot, or their day changed, and by the time they remembered it felt too awkward to call. A reminder before the appointment fixes that, but only if it lets them reschedule with a reply instead of asking them to call you back.

"You're booked with Maya on Saturday at 10 AM. Reply to move it or cancel." A client who can move their appointment by text will move it, and the earlier they do, the easier the slot is to fill. A client who has to call will put it off, and then just not show up. The difference between a rescheduled client and a no-show is usually one reply.

## The bottom line

A last-minute cancellation is not a policy problem. It is a speed problem. Keep a waitlist that knows who wanted which stylist and which day, text the right person within minutes of the gap opening, and make it easy for clients to move their booking instead of ghosting it.

Do that, and the empty chair stops being a lost morning. It becomes the slot your waitlist was waiting for.
