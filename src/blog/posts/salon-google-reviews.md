Someone looking for a new salon does two things before they call. They check how close you are, and they read your Google reviews. The star rating decides whether they call at all, and the words inside the reviews decide whether they trust you with their hair.

That is why reviews matter more for salons than for most local businesses. Nobody picks a colorist on price alone. They pick the place where other clients say a stylist listened, fixed a color another salon got wrong, or gave them the best cut they have had in years.

A review that says "Maya finally got my balayage right" does that work. A review that says "great salon" does not.

## When to ask: the same day, while it is fresh

Most salons ask for reviews at the wrong time, or never ask at all. A card on the counter gets ignored. An email three days later lands when the visit is already forgotten. A request at the next visit is a month too late.

The time that works is the same day as the visit. The client is still catching their reflection in shop windows, and the experience is exactly what the review should describe. Ask then, and the client can answer in thirty seconds. Ask later, and you are asking them to remember a feeling they have already moved on from.

That is also why the ask has to be one tap. A link that opens the review form directly, with nothing to download and no account to create first. Every extra step loses people who were perfectly happy to leave a review.

## What to say: name the stylist

The single biggest difference between a review that helps you and one that does not is whether it names a person. "Great salon" tells a stranger nothing. "Jess listened to exactly what I wanted and nailed it" tells them exactly who to ask for and what to expect.

So the ask itself should name the stylist from the real visit that just happened: "Thanks for coming in to see Maya today!" not "Thanks for visiting." The client is not being asked to write a review of a business. They are being asked to say thank you to the person who did their hair, and the review writes itself from there.

Ask every client. Do not pick who gets asked based on how you think the visit went. The clients you are unsure about are often the ones whose honest review helps you most, and picking only the happy ones is exactly how a salon ends up with a row of identical five-star reviews that nobody believes.

## What to do with an unhappy client

Some visits do not go well, and the worst thing you can do is pretend they did not happen. A client who had a bad visit and hears nothing from you is the client who writes the one-star review that stays at the top of your page for a year.

The fix is not to hide the review ask from unhappy clients. It is to give them a private way back to you as well. When a client says the visit was not right, that message should reach the owner directly, so the owner can fix it, offer to make it right, and hear what actually happened before the client tells Google instead.

That is a conversation, not a filter. The owner hears about the problem while it is still fixable, and the client feels heard instead of ignored. Most of the time, that is the whole recovery.

## How to reply: every review gets an answer

A review is not finished when the client posts it. It is finished when you reply to it, by name, naming the stylist again. A short reply to a five-star review tells the next person reading it that the owner is paying attention. A calm, specific reply to a critical review tells them even more.

Reply to all of them, not just the good ones. A page full of unanswered reviews reads like a salon that stopped caring. A page where the owner answers every review reads like a salon that will care about you too.

The reply does not need to be long. Thank them, name the stylist, and say one true thing about the visit. That is enough, and it is the part most salons skip entirely.

## The bottom line

Getting more reviews is not a begging problem. It is a timing problem and a naming problem. Ask the same day, while the experience is fresh. Name the stylist, so the review says something a stranger can trust. Give unhappy clients a private way back to you, and answer every review that comes in.

Do that after every visit, and the reviews build themselves, one real visit at a time.
